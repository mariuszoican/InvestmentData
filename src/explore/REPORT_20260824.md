**Prepared for:** co-author review. **Status:** exploratory; not for circulation.

## 1. What this session is (and how it differs from the Prolific study)

This is a single in-person lab session with students from FNCE 449/668: **37 completers** (of 39 starters), **all assigned to the treatment condition** — `include_control: 0`, so there is **no control group** in this session. Each participant played 2 training + **18 main rounds** (vs 12 on Prolific), with a **guaranteed 9 paid-data / 9 free-data split** in random order (vs a 2/3–1/3 random draw per round on Prolific). The data fee is **E$5** on an E$100 endowment; 50% of rounds are objectively informative (corr ≈ 0.75 between lagged imbalance and next return); the belief question is asked only when the participant sees data. Two further protocol changes relative to the paper draft: the forecast bonus band is **±5pp** (was ±1pp) and the chart-series display order is shuffled independently per participant.

Two identification consequences:

1. **No selection-free benchmark.** Without a control group, the "Treated" coefficient from the paper's specifications cannot be estimated. All identification is within-treatment: the sunk-cost effect comes from the random paid/free draw *within participants who chose to pay*, and the selection effect from comparing choosers vs decliners *in free rounds* (where everyone sees data).
2. **Power.** With 37 clusters, minimum detectable effects are roughly 2.5–3× the Prolific point estimates for most outcomes. Null results below are mostly *uninformative about* the paper's effects — with one notable exception (H3, Section 5). I report 95% CIs against the Prolific benchmarks throughout.

### Sample vs the Prolific cohort

| Statistic | Lab 2026-08-24 (n=37) | Prolific (n=771) |
|---|---|---|
| Age (mean) | 24.8 | 45.7 (median 45) |
| Female | 30% | 50% |
| Financial quiz score | 0.80 | 0.76 |
| Overconfidence (self/10 − quiz) | **−0.27** | −0.21 |
| Finance course | 59% | 33% |
| Trading experience | 57% | 64% |
| Choose to pay | **75%** | 56% |
| Mean investment share | 44.4% | 45.8% |
| Belief data informative (overall) | **59%** | 72% |
| … when truly informative | **84%** | 75% |
| … when truly uninformative | **35%** | 68% |

Two headline differences. First, take-up is much higher (75% vs 56%); 22% of participants pay in *all* 18 rounds and nobody never pays. Second — the most striking feature of this session — **belief discrimination is an order of magnitude sharper**: the informative–uninformative belief gap is **49pp** (84% vs 35%) against **7pp** on Prolific (75% vs 68%). Finance students are simply much better at reading the charts, and they show none of the Prolific sample's baseline credulity about noise (35% vs 68% belief in uninformative rounds). Overall classification accuracy is 74.3%, nearly identical across the literacy split (75.0% high-quiz vs 73.4% low-quiz — both well above the Prolific averages).

## 2. H1 — Payment and beliefs: **no sunk-cost distortion detected**

Specification: OLS of `belief_informative` on `Paid × Uninformative`, `Paid`, `Informative`, `Choose to pay`, round index, last return, last imbalance; SEs clustered by participant (sample: 580 participant-rounds with data access).

| Coefficient | All | Participant FE | High quiz | Low quiz | Prolific |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paid × Not informative | −0.01 (0.06) | −0.04 (0.06) | −0.02 (0.07) | −0.01 (0.11) | **+0.08*** |
| Paid round | −0.00 (0.04) | 0.01 (0.04) | −0.01 (0.05) | 0.01 (0.07) | −0.02 |
| Informative round | **0.49*** (0.06) | **0.48*** (0.06) | **0.50*** (0.07) | **0.47*** (0.11) | 0.10*** |
| Choose to pay | −0.02 (0.07) | 0.09 (0.08) | 0.08 (0.09) | **−0.16**** (0.07) | 0.13*** |

- The sunk-cost interaction is a tight zero: **−0.013, 95% CI [−0.13, +0.10]**. The CI *includes* the Prolific +0.08, so this is not a rejection of H1 — but the point estimate offers no support either. Raw cells tell the same story: belief in uninformative rounds is 35.9% free vs 33.9% paid.
- The `Informative` coefficient (+0.49 vs +0.10 on Prolific) confirms the discrimination result: these subjects extract the true round type from the data far more reliably, leaving little "interpretive slack" for a sunk cost to act on. This is consistent with List (2011)-style attenuation of endowment effects among experienced agents, and suggests **the belief channel requires ambiguity to operate** — a point worth making in the paper.
- One anomaly: among low-quiz students, *choosers* are 16pp **less** likely to call data informative in free rounds (p=0.02) — the opposite of the Prolific selection effect. With 16 low-quiz participants I would not over-read this.

## 3. H2 — Payment and forecast sensitivity: **null on payment; strong baseline responsiveness; clean placebo**

Sample: believer rounds (`belief_informative = 1`, N=344). Forecast regressed on imbalance, its interactions with `Paid` and `Choose to pay`, and controls.

- **Baseline sensitivity is strong and correct in sign:** 0.457 per pp of imbalance (p<0.001), i.e. ≈ **4.1pp per 1σ (9pp) of imbalance** — more than twice the Prolific responsiveness (1.85 per σ). Note the rational full-information benchmark is a slope of 1; even these students underreact, consistent with Andries et al. (2025).
- **Paid × Imbalance ≈ 0** (0.013, se 0.108). In relative terms the CI spans ±46% of the baseline slope, so the Prolific +31% cannot be ruled out — genuinely underpowered.
- **Placebo passes:** among non-believer rounds the imbalance slope is 0.035 (se 0.20), indistinguishable from zero. Beliefs and forecasts are internally consistent.
- **Novel selection pattern:** among high-quiz believers, `Choose to pay × Imbalance` = **+0.43 (p=0.01)** — sophisticated students who habitually buy data respond ~2.5× more strongly to it than sophisticated non-buyers (0.29 baseline). On Prolific, this interaction was ≈ 0/negative. Suggests that in a high-literacy pool, selection into purchase reflects genuine intent to *use* the signal.
- Forecasts load on last return negatively here (−1.6, p=0.04) — mild *contrarian* extrapolation, in contrast to the strong positive extrapolation (+3.96) on Prolific. Another marker of the sample's sophistication.

**Noise overfitting (forecast errors, objectively uninformative rounds, N=293).** Mean |forecast − 15| is **7.00 free vs 8.03 paid** — a raw gap of **+1.03pp, remarkably close to the Prolific +1.08pp** — but with 37 clusters the regression coefficient (+0.53, se 0.80) is far from significance; CI [−1.1, +2.2] comfortably includes the Prolific estimate. Direction: consistent; evidence: weak. The gap is concentrated among low-quiz participants (+1.52, se 1.27), directionally mirroring nothing in Prolific (where it was flat across literacy) — again, small cells.

## 4. H3/H4 — Investment levels and pass-through (IV)

2SLS on believer rounds (N=344): investment share on instrumented forecast and forecast × Paid (instruments: last imbalance and its Paid interaction), with Paid, Choose-to-pay, round, last return (interactions centered at sample means so `Paid` is the level effect at the average forecast). First stage is strong (imbalance t≈7.9). SEs clustered by participant.

| Coefficient | All | High quiz | Low quiz | Prolific |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Return forecast (IV) | **2.36*** (0.43) | **2.51*** (0.56) | **1.77** ** (0.63) | 2.48*** |
| Forecast × Paid | −0.58 (0.45) | −0.47 (0.53) | −0.29 (0.69) | **−0.37*** |
| Paid round | 0.32 (3.07) | 2.45 (3.50) | −4.80 (5.01) | **+7.16*** |
| Choose to pay | 14.71* (8.55) | 6.74 (9.23) | **28.00** ** (10.02) | 2.37 |

- **Pass-through replicates beautifully:** 2.36 vs Prolific's 2.48 — a 1pp higher informed forecast raises investment ~2.4pp. This is the most robust cross-sample regularity in the design.
- **H4 direction, not significance:** −0.58 (p=0.20) vs Prolific −0.37; a ~25% elasticity reduction in paid rounds. CI includes both zero and the Prolific value.
- **H3 fails, and this null has teeth.** The level effect of payment is **+0.32pp with 95% CI ≈ [−5.9, +6.6] — which excludes the Prolific +7.16pp**. Moreover, the Prolific effect was concentrated in *low-literacy* subjects (+14.6pp); here the low-quiz point estimate is *negative* (−4.8). This is the one place where the lab data actively push back against the paper's estimate rather than merely lacking power. Two readings: (i) the sunk-cost investment channel, like the belief channel, is a phenomenon of unsophisticated investors and largely absent in a finance-trained pool — consistent with the paper's own heterogeneity story if "low quiz" *within this sample* (median 0.83) is still high in absolute terms; (ii) protocol differences (9/9 deterministic split, ±5pp bonus band, 18 rounds) matter. I lean toward (i): even the lab's low-quiz group scores ~0.70, above the Prolific *median*.
- **The `Choose to pay` coefficient (+14.7pp*) is intent, not selection.** Decomposing pay choice into between-person (habitual pay rate) and within-person (this round's deviation) components: the between part is noise (+15.8, se 21.2, p=0.46; person-level corr of pay rate with mean investment is 0.16, p=0.34), while the within part is **+5.9pp (p=0.03)** — in rounds where a participant opts to buy relative to their own average, they invest ~6pp more. Buying data and intending to trade are a joint round-level decision; there is no stable "payer type" who takes more risk.
- Raw means agree: believers invest 48.0% in paid vs 42.1% in free rounds (+5.9pp), but the gap reflects this round-level intent composition, not the random payment draw.

## 5. H5 — Who pays? **Selection reverses in a high-literacy pool**

Round-level LPM of pay choice on standardized characteristics (round FE, SEs clustered by participant):

| | Lab (std. coef) | Prolific |
|---|---|---|
| Overconfidence | **−0.09*** (p=0.098) | **+0.05*** |
| Financial quiz | −0.08 (p=0.28) | **+0.06*** |
| Risk aversion (HL switch) | −0.05 (p=0.26) | −0.02 |
| Female | −0.21 (p=0.13) | −0.01 |

Both Prolific selection results flip sign. In this (on average *under*confident: mean OC −0.27) student pool, the more overconfident students buy *less* data (raw: 70% above-median OC vs 80% below-median), and quiz score does not predict purchase. A plausible reading: among students who nearly all recognize the data's objective value (75% take-up), residual variation in purchase is driven by self-perceived *lack* of skill — data as a crutch — rather than by overconfident data-chasing. The gender gap in take-up (−21pp for women) is much larger than on Prolific and worth tracking in future lab sessions.

**Where selection *does* show up: competence and usage, not credulity or risk appetite.** Decomposing pay choice into between-person (habitual pay rate) and within-person components:

- *Beliefs:* habitual payers are, if anything, **less** likely to call data informative (between: −0.18, p≈0.06; within: ≈0, n.s.) — the reverse of Prolific's +13pp chooser premium. Consistently, a person's pay rate correlates **+0.38 (p=0.02)** with belief classification accuracy: here the habitual buyers are the better discriminators.
- *Forecast usage:* among believers, responsiveness to imbalance rises steeply with habitual pay rate (Imbalance × between-pay = **+0.53, p=0.01**, on a base slope of 0.22; within and paid interactions ≈ 0). Always-payers respond roughly 3.4× more strongly to the signal than never-payers — they buy it because they use it.
- *Performance:* despite classifying better and responding more, habitual payers earn no more (corr of pay rate with round payoff +0.06, n.s.) and make no smaller forecast errors (−0.15, n.s.).

Caveat: between-person power is weak — 37 people with a compressed pay-rate distribution (median 0.89; between-person variance 0.078 vs within 0.112), and at 75% take-up decliners are a small selected group.

**Purchase dynamics (new evidence, not in the paper):**

- **Round 1 enthusiasm collapse:** 94.6% pay in main round 1, dropping to 51.4% in round 2, then stabilizing at ~75%. Participants appear to try the product once, reassess after the first feedback screen, and settle into a habit.
- **Strong state dependence:** paying-and-seeing-data last round raises the probability of paying this round by **+20pp (p=0.001)**, controlling for round trend and last round's outcomes; there is no extra reaction to whether last round's data turned out informative (−0.02, n.s.). Purchase behaves like a *habit*, not like Bayesian updating on realized data quality — a genuinely new pattern worth a figure in the paper if it survives pooling.

## 6. Other patterns worth flagging

1. **Paying for data was money-losing.** Mean end-of-round wealth: **E$107.7 in free-data rounds vs E$101.9 in paid rounds** (E$104.3 when declining in a paid round and trading blind). The E$5.9 gap slightly exceeds the fee: even *gross of the fee*, payers extracted no extra value (101.9 + 5 = 106.9 < 107.7). The fee was calibrated as fair for an optimizing CRRA investor; actual subjects do not recoup it — consistent with Chapkovski et al. (2025b)'s willingness-to-pay result, now with realized-payoff evidence.
2. **Escalating risk-taking:** investment share climbs steadily from 28% (round 1) to ~50% by round 12 (round trend ≈ +1.2pp/round; the paper found +2.3 over 12 rounds). Worth checking against the house-money interpretation when pooling.
3. **Payment weakly inflates forecast *confidence*** (+0.12 on a 1–5 scale, p=0.089) but not belief confidence (+0.03, n.s.). The confidence elicitations are collected by the platform but unused in the paper — with the pooled sample they could give a second, continuous measure of the endowment-in-beliefs channel.
4. **No sunk-cost spillover to non-believers:** among rounds where the participant deems data uninformative, paid vs free investment is −4.5pp (n.s.) — the "justify the expense by trading" channel does not operate through people who think the data is noise, consistent with the model's conditioning on perceived usefulness.
5. Investment behavior is close to the Merton benchmark on average (44.4% vs 43.4% optimal baseline share) — remarkable calibration in levels, as on Prolific.

## 7. Bottom line and recommendations

| Hypothesis | Prolific | This lab session |
|---|---|---|
| H1 belief distortion (Paid × Uninf) | +8pp*** | ≈ 0 (n.s.; CI includes +8pp) |
| H2 forecast sensitivity (× Paid) | +31%*** | ≈ 0 (n.s.; CI includes +31%) |
| H2b noise overfitting | +1.08pp** | +1.03pp raw, +0.53 (n.s.) |
| H3 investment level (Paid) | +7.2pp*** | +0.3pp; **CI excludes +7.2** |
| H4 pass-through (Forecast × Paid) | −0.37*** | −0.58 (n.s., right sign) |
| H5 selection: overconfidence | +5pp** | **−9pp*** (sign flip) |
| Pass-through of forecasts (benchmark) | 2.48*** | **2.36*** (replicates) |

1. **The mechanical economics replicate; the behavioral distortions largely do not.** Forecast→investment pass-through, belief–forecast internal consistency, and the placebo all come through cleanly. The sunk-cost effects (H1–H4) are absent or undetectable in a sample that is dramatically better at classifying signal vs noise.
2. **This is a *useful* non-replication.** It sharpens the paper's heterogeneity story into a falsifiable claim: payment distorts beliefs and trading only where subjects cannot confidently classify the data (Prolific: 68% believe noise; lab: 35%). Consider adding the lab session as an "expert benchmark" appendix — the H3 CI exclusion and the H5 sign flip are the two results that genuinely qualify the external validity of the Prolific estimates, in the direction the paper already argues (sophistication protects).
3. **Do not pool naively.** Different design (9/9 vs 2/3 draw, 18 vs 12 rounds, ±5 vs ±1 forecast band, no control arm) and a very different population. If pooled, interact everything with a session/population dummy.
4. **For future lab sessions:** (i) include the control arm (`include_control: 1`) — the current session cannot estimate the elicitation effect; (ii) recruit below-median-literacy students if the goal is to test the sunk-cost channel where the paper predicts it lives; (iii) keep the confidence elicitations — the forecast-confidence result (p=0.089, n=37) is promising.
5. **Caveats:** 37 clusters (cluster-robust SEs are borderline; wild-bootstrap p-values would be the careful next step), one session, one classroom, no preregistered lab-specific power analysis. Everything here is exploratory.
